Stain vs. Paint for Your Deck or Fence: What’s the Difference?

Your deck and fence live outside all year. They take the summer sun, the fall rain, the winter snow and the spring thaw, and bare wood does not last long under that kind of weather. It turns grey, cracks and eventually rots. A protective finish is not optional. The real question is which one: stain or paint?

Both protect wood, but they work in different ways, look different and age differently. Here is how they compare, and how to choose the right one for your backyard. For a closer look at the work itself, see our page on deck and fence staining in Toronto.

How Stain and Paint Work

The main difference is where the finish sits.

Stain soaks into the wood. It carries pigment and protective ingredients below the surface, so the grain and texture of the wood stay visible. Stains come in a range of opacities, from clear and semi-transparent, which show the most grain, to solid, which hides the grain but still shows the texture.

Paint sits on top of the wood. It forms a solid film that covers the grain completely and gives you a uniform color. That film is what makes paint so good at hiding flaws, and it is also what makes it vulnerable when it fails.

How They Look

If you paid for cedar or another good-looking wood, stain lets you see it. A semi-transparent stain adds warmth and color while the natural pattern of the wood shows through. This is the classic look most people picture when they think of a deck.

Paint gives you a clean, solid color, and almost any color you want. It can match your trim or your house and it hides knots, stains and mismatched boards. On an older fence with repairs and patches, that can be a real advantage.

Solid stain sits in between: full color like paint, but with the wood's texture still visible.

How They Wear

This is where the two differ most, especially in Ontario.

Wood moves. It swells when it is wet and shrinks when it dries, and our freeze and thaw cycles make that movement worse. Stain, being inside the wood, moves with it. When stain wears, it fades gradually. It does not peel.

Paint is a film on the surface. When water gets behind it, through a crack, a nail hole or the end of a board, the film lifts. That is when you see peeling and flaking, and on a deck floor, where people walk and snow sits, it tends to happen sooner.

For this reason, stain is usually the preferred choice for deck floors. On vertical surfaces like fences and railings, where water runs off and nobody walks, paint and solid stain hold up much better.

How Much Maintenance Each One Needs

Neither finish lasts forever. The difference is in what the next round of work looks like.

With stain, maintenance is simpler. When the color fades or water stops beading on the surface, the wood is cleaned and a fresh coat goes on. There is usually no heavy scraping, because there is no film to remove.

With paint, a good job can go longer before it needs attention, especially on a fence. But once paint starts to peel, the loose paint has to be scraped and sanded before repainting. Skipping that step only traps the problem under a new coat.

How often you need to re-stain depends on the type of wood, the product, how much sun the surface gets and how much traffic it sees. A deck in full sun with kids and a dog will need attention sooner than a shaded fence.

Can You Switch From One to the Other?

Going from stain to paint is fairly straightforward: the wood is cleaned, primed and painted.

Going from paint back to stain is hard. Stain needs to soak in, and it cannot do that through paint. The old paint has to be removed completely, which means stripping and sanding the whole surface. It can be done, but it is a much bigger job. This is worth knowing before you paint a deck for the first time.

Which One Should You Choose?

Stain is usually the better choice when:

  • The surface is a deck floor or stairs that get walked on
  • The wood is in good shape and worth showing
  • You want easier maintenance in the years ahead
  • You prefer a natural, warm look

Paint or solid stain can be the better choice when:

  • The surface is a fence, railing or trim
  • The wood is older, patched or uneven in color
  • You want a specific solid color to match your house
  • The surface has already been painted

Prep Matters More Than the Product

Whichever you choose, the result depends on what happens before the first coat. The wood needs to be cleaned, old flaking finish removed, rough areas sanded, and everything must be completely dry. New wood often needs time to dry out before it can absorb stain properly.

Outdoor work also depends on the weather. Staining is best done on dry days with mild temperatures, from late spring to early fall, which is why the season fills up quickly.
